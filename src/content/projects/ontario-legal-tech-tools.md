## The context

I worked with an Ontario legal-tech startup building software for personal injury law workflows. The users were legal professionals who needed tools to support case work without adding another difficult system to learn.

This case study focuses on the web-product side of that engagement.

## Improving the product experience

I redesigned the homepage to make the product easier to understand and navigate. The work used TypeScript, Next.js, and TailwindCSS, with attention to hierarchy, clearer entry points, and a more consistent interface.

In legal software, visual polish only helps when it supports the task. The design had to make the next action obvious to staff who were using the product to get work done.

## Documentation for real adoption

I wrote documentation for two tools used by non-technical legal staff. The goal was to explain the workflow in the language of the task, not the language of the implementation.

That meant documenting where to begin, what information the tool expected, and how to understand what happened next.

## What I learned

This work reinforced that internal software has two interfaces: the product and the explanation around it. A technically complete feature can still fail if the people using it cannot confidently move through the workflow.

The strongest improvements came from treating interface design, implementation, and documentation as one adoption problem rather than three separate deliverables.
