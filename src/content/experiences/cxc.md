## How I got here

I did not join CxC to put “Co-Chair” on a LinkedIn line. I joined because Waterloo’s data and AI crowd is huge, and for a long time the biggest weekend on campus still felt like it was talking to a narrower slice of that crowd than it could.

CxC is Waterloo’s largest AI hackathon. Fifth year now. Hundreds of students, one campus weekend, mentors walking the floor, and a judging room that fills with demos that barely slept. I co-chair it. That means I care about the brief, the sponsors, the workshops, and whether a first-year who showed up nervous on Friday still wants to come back on Sunday.

## What was broken

Older briefs leaned toward a specific kind of project. Fine if you already knew the genre. Rough if you had a weird idea, a half-trained model, or a dataset you actually cared about and did not want to force into last year’s shape.

People also showed up, built in a bubble, and left. The weekend worked as a shipping contest. It did not always work as a place to meet the person who would unblock you at 1am, or the company you might actually want to talk to after the closing ceremony.

## What we changed

### The brief

This year we opened it. No project-type lock. Predictions on a dataset, a model you trained yourself, a small data-science site that does one job well: all of it counts. The point was to stop rewarding the team that mirrored the prompt the hardest and start rewarding the team that shipped something real.

That decision sounds small on a landing page. On the floor it changed who felt invited. I watched teams pitch ideas they would have self-rejected under a tighter brief, then actually build them.

### The weekend around the build

A hackathon that is only laptops and silence is a study hall with worse chairs. We filled the gaps: workshops people could walk into without a perfect background, company booths that were not just swag tables, and socials that gave introverts a second door into the room.

My job in that mix was less “announce from a stage” and more “make the room work.” Pair someone stuck on setup with someone who just finished it. Move a workshop when the room double-booked. Notice when a booth was empty because the power strip died, not because nobody cared.

### Late night

If you have never run a hackathon past midnight, it is hard to explain how physical the role gets. Cables. Room swaps. A demo laptop that will not join the AV. Someone whose teammate went home and who is quietly spiraling about presenting alone.

I stayed on the floor for that part on purpose. Judging only works if the path to the demo still exists at 2am. Keeping the room loud in the right way (curious, not chaotic) is character work as much as logistics: stay calm, stay useful, do not make a tired builder feel stupid for asking the same question twice.

## What I took from it

Co-chairing CxC taught me that “AI event” is the easy label. The real product is whether people leave feeling like they belong in the room they just built in. Opening the brief was a product decision. Walking the floor at 2am was the delivery.

I am still in the role. The event keeps growing. The part I care about most has not changed: a weekend where Waterloo’s AI and data people get to build what they actually want, and still find each other while they do it.
