## How I landed here

Hack the 6ix is Toronto’s biggest student hackathon. I came in on business development for the June 2026 weekend, which in practice meant sponsorships: find partners early, pitch something they would actually buy, then make sure the weekend did not embarrass the relationship we just sold.

I already knew the builder side of hackathons from CxC. HT6 asked for the other half. Talking to companies before the venue is decorated. Translating “we want brand presence” into a booth, a workshop slot, and a package that does not fall apart under a real budget conversation.

## The work before the weekend

### Outreach that starts too early on purpose

Sponsorship outreach started months out. Cold emails. Decks. Follow-ups while most people still thought of June as “later.” That timeline matters. If you wait until the event feels close, the good partners already spent their student-marketing budget somewhere else.

A lot of the job was repetition with taste. Same story, rewritten for a title sponsor versus a smaller booth. Same weekend, different reasons to care. I got better at noticing when a reply was a soft no disguised as “circle back,” and when it was a real maybe that needed one more specific ask.

### Closing Base44

Base44 came on as title sponsor. That did not happen in one heroic email. It took a few rounds on booth presence, branding, and what a title package should feel like when someone walks into the venue: visible without being loud in a way that crowds out the builders.

I cared about that detail because I have been the person on the floor when a “title” logo is everywhere and the actual experience for students is worse. The package had to look strong in a deck and still feel good at check-in.

### Fitting different partners

Not every company wants the same thing. FDM, Next Canada, and other partners needed packages that matched different budgets and goals: hiring signal, product awareness, workshop time with builders. I worked those conversations directly instead of forcing everyone through one rigid tier and hoping it stuck.

## On site

Closing a sponsor is not the end of business development. It is the start of the part where you can still lose the relationship in public.

I lined up sponsor workshops and then lived in the on-site grind: check-in lines, booth setup, room swaps when AV failed, walking the floor when a partner could not find power or a workshop host was late. That work is unglamorous. It is also how you prove the pitch was not fiction.

When something broke, I would rather be the person who already knows the room layout than the person refreshing Slack from a quiet corner. Sponsors remember the weekend they were taken care of. Students remember whether the floor still worked while they were trying to ship.

## What shifted for me

HT6 pulled me out of “I can run the builder room” and into “I can sell the room, then protect what I sold.” Cold outreach, package design, title negotiation, and the Saturday logistics loop are the same job at different zoom levels.

I left that weekend more comfortable starting conversations with companies I did not already know, and more stubborn about follow-through after the contract. A sponsorship that looks good in a spreadsheet and feels bad on the floor is not a win. I want both.
